Anonymous @ 22 Jan 2003 — 12:15am
Basically, there is no "complete listing": you can take it as far as you want to. The ct, st, and sp for example are not in use anymore because, due to changing in tastes, most people today find them disruptive in the flow of reading. The sz has been thrown out of modern German, and I haven't seen many examples using the ft and fft-ligatures in Petr van Blokland's Proforma yet. I'll try to post a couple of examples later on.

Anonymous @ 22 Jan 2003 — 1:29pm
I don't know of a complete listing. I think there isn't yet a font that explores _every_ ligature possibility. [Mantinia](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042445/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/fontbureau/mantinia/mantiniacc/charmap.html) has some nice cap [examples](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042445/http:/www.fontbureau.com/fonts/font_frames.tpl?cart=10432214612728759&fontname=Mantinia). [Mrs. Eaves](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042445/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/emigre/mrs-eaves/) sports so many ligatures they are housed in seperate sets and sold with a [Ligature Maker](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042445/http:/www.emigre.com/EFfeature.php?sp=142) app. And for an unconventional method of connecting letterforms see the inventive [Blue Island](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042445/http:/typography.net/type/b_island.htm). Most common ligatures (off the top of my head): fl, ff, fi, ffl, ffi
