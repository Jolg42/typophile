"humanist sans" - 1st use of label?

I am trying to trace the history of the use of the term “humanist sans serif.” It seems pretty indisputable that Gill Sans is the first of this kind, but I have found that, though from early on Gill Sans is promoted as a sans of a different kind to the contemporary geometric sans-serifs from Germany, “humanist” doesn’t seem to have been the word used to name this “different kind.”

A companion question (which actually may go pretty far in helping to answer the first) is when was “humanist” first used to describe specifically ANY lettering (that is, seriffed types or script)? B. L. Ullman in 1960 still opines that humanistic script has been deplorably neglected in scholarship, but cites Morison’s writings of the early 1940s as the “leading treatment.” Was “humanist” used in any of the turn-of-the-twentieth-century books? (I have Theodore Low De Vinne’s 1900 Practice of Typography here and I don’t see the term used.)

So: what’s the earliest you’ve seen “humanist” used to refer to a sans-serif type?

And what’s the earliest you’ve seen “humanist” used to refer to any lettering?

Thanks for any help you can provide!

How to obtain unavailable fonts?

I’m a typophile. Yes, a true addict. I will freely (and guiltily) admit to having in my possession several hundreds of free and commercial, legally and illegally-acquired fonts that I will probably never, ever use – from large foundaries and small. Fonts in TrueType, PostScript Type 1, and OpenType formats.

But there are some that I’m absolutely in love with. I use the Scala Sans font family in my resume. This family is so much easier to read than TrueType Arial and its forced variations. I know this one is commercial, and I will buy them all as soon as it’s financially feasible.

I also use some of the fonts in documents I create for the D&D game. I have not distributed any of my work (aside from my resume), but even privately I like to make the documents look as much as if they had been downloaded from the Wizards of the Coast website as I can. These fonts are no secret to those interested in the typography and design of the D&D product line.

Eventually, I would like to release some of my work on the Internet. And this would actually require specific fonts, which are variations of the commercial fonts I have already used. These variations are not available, freely (as in legally free) or commercially, anywhere. For instance, the font Celestia Antiqua Medium, used in countless PDFs on the Wizards of the Coast website and in their books, is not available for sale. Dactyl, also, is conspicuously missing. And while Celestia Antiqua Regular and Ptera suit for private use, they aren’t the same.

My question, then, is “What would be the best methods to legitimately acquire those fonts I can’t get through normal channels?” Remember, I want to own and be able to use these fonts in my work, and even though they will likely never be used for profit of any sort, I’d like to keep that option open. Still, I want the quality of the actual fonts (not ripped from PDF or PS files - I have some dignity, as well as a decent eye for aesthetics).
