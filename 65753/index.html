Ain’t is spelled that way by Oxford Concise Dictionary. Fowler defends “ain’t” or “an’t” as an abbreviation for “am not I”, as in “ain’t I pretty?” We tend to say “aren’t I pretty”, which is even less defensible.

I think what Fowler was probably getting at in William’s comment above, is that _ain’t_ completes a very productive syntactic paradigm in English (contractions of pronominal + be + negation): You are not; You’re not; You aren’t He is not; She’s not; It isn’t We are not; We’re not; We aren’t They are not; They’re not; They aren’t I am not; I’m not; \*I amn’t / ?I ain’t Not that most folks use _ain’t_ in precisely that manner—but that’s what Fowler was suggesting over the ridiculously ungrammatical _aren’t I_. <cite>and everyone agrees on the meaning… </cite> I think that may actually depend on where you hail from. Anonymous @ 30.Dec.2009 12.05am
The prescriptivists of the 19th century forced “ain’t” out of standard speech. My guess is that the relatively obscure relationship between “am not” and the contraction bothered some people (the form had formerly been “an’t”). Then probably in reaction to this, people began to use “ain’t” in other meanings—“isn’t”, “hasn’t”, etc. More on this: [http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain’t](http://web.archive.org/web/20100103025716/http:/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain't) Now the stigma attached to the word is so great, and its use so variable in meaning in different groups, that it’s hard to see this come back in its original, logical meaning. The prescriptivists ruined a perfectly good word, innit?
