Blackletter Ligatures

Why do i have such a thing for ligatures? I don’t know. Anyhow, i’m wondering if we have an expert on blackletter ligatures in the house. I know that the half r can follow “round” letters (b d o p) to form ligatures. I know that the “e” can be attached to these round letters as well. i’m familiar with the double-p ligature. and then there are always your f-ligs and longs-ligs. then you have your historical German ck and ch ligs, which i’m informed are no longer relevant. And then there’s tz, which is used in German. Are there more? I know that potentially everything could be a ligature, but i’m talking about fairly standard blackletter ligature conventions here. anyhow, if anyone knows of any other interesting combinations, pipe up!

"historical German ck and ch ligs, which i’m informed are no longer relevant!" Ahm no sir! If you set German texts in Fraktur or any black letter typeface, you have to stuck to the old traditional rules since no one has change it. This means at least the long\_s and hopefully the common ligatures. The round r is outdated since the time of Fleischmann. Common ligatures are: c\_h c\_k longs\_c\_h longs\_longs longs\_i longs\_t longs\_z (germandbls) f\_f f\_i f\_l f\_t t\_z l\_l Even more ligatures are possible. Have a look at the original lead typeface of Klingspor Gotisch (not the Linotype digi version). But anyway, dump, naive people set even German texts like common texts today - and it looks awful. But were is no standard of a Fraktur codepage or a Fraktur keyboard layout. Only a pseudo standard "Dfr" (Deutsche Frakur) is available - setting up the long\_s and its ligatures on false glyphs on a standard codepage. John, how is the status of the Fraktron project? In the [Christian Gothic](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042425/http:/typophile.com/node/9245) (Imprimerie Nationale, 1902), I'm working on, the complete set of ligatures is: ff, fi, ffi, ct, tt, ll Note that fl, in particular, is not ligated. Obviously, fj is uncommon in French, but presumably it would follow the same rules as fi. Additional variants include: final s, final n, initial v. There are also final-swash e, t, r. When I release the font, all these ligatures and variants will be included. I'm still undecided which ones to make optional, though - I think that'll take more experience using the type in present-day settings.

Paul, there are literally thousands of scribal ligatures used in the original blackletter calligraphy and in Gutenberg's own B42 type. The most common is any letter with a bar over it indicating a doubled letter. For an exhaustive survey see Ulrico Hoepli's _Lexicon Abbreviaturarum_. Anonymous @ 13 Aug 2005 — 5:51pm
John, the _Dizionario di Abbreviature..._ is by Cappelli; Hoepli was just the publisher. It is often referred to simply as 'Cappelli'. Another good reference book for scribal contractions is _The Record Interpreter_ by Charles Trice-Martin. This is more specifically British in its focus, being intended as an aid to deciphering public rolls, parish records, etc. [The equivalent volume for Greek is T.W. Allen's _Abbreviations in Greek Manuscripts_, which is helpfully included in the more recent collection _Abbreviations in Greek inscriptions, papyri, manuscripts and early printed books_ edited by Oikonomides.] There is also what looks like a really useful electronic dictionary, [Abbreviationes](http://web.archive.org/web/20150322042425/http:/www.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/philosophy/projects/abbrev.htm). I have not used this, largely because it is only available for the Mac, but it seems very promising.
