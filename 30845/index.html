Anonymous @ 1 Feb 2007 — 10:13am
The Baltic languages and Romanian use letters with a comma accent below them. The Turkish language uses cedilla. The Unicode Standard was admittedly wrong when assuming that the Baltic letters such as "G with comma accent" are letters with cedillas. This is why they named them "Latin capital letter G with cedilla" and created a canonical decomposition into G and cedilla. The Unicode Consortium now knows that they were wrong, but for compatibility reasons they were not able to change the decomposition or the names. The only thing they revised is the sample glyphs in the Standard. Adobe is not bound by backwards compatibility issues in glyph naming so Adobe was able to change the old AGL name "Gcedilla" to a new AGLFN name "Gcommaaccent". This was done deliberately, after a careful review of all the issues involved. Of course the Unicodes are still the same for those characters, and the keyboard layouts you tried use those Unicodes. Some older fonts (that you may have tried to preview the results of your typing) may also be wrong in that they include cedillas in place of commaaccents in the design of those glyphs. But this is a problem with those fonts, and some of them (e.g. Arial) will likely be changed in the next version of Windows. The issue of keyboard mapping has nothing to do with glyph design or the naming. Keyboard layouts generate Unicodes. The fonts map Unicodes to glyphs. Glyph names are only used as an auxiliary mechanism. Even though the Unicode names and canonical decomposition is wrong, it is fixed and cannot be changed. But each font is separate and the glyph design should reflect the typographic traditions and not the mistakes of some gremium. To sum up: 1. All Baltic glyphs should use commaaccents, and use the names like Ncommaaccent, Kcommaaccent, Gcommaaccent etc., but of course they map to Unicodes that in the Unicode Standard have names such as "Latin capital letter N with cedilla", and decompose into N + cedilla. That's fine, and that's the correct (at least from the standards point of view) way of handling things. 2. The T character has two instances in the standard: Latin capital letter T with cedilla (in the 01xx section) and Latin capital letter T with commaaccent (in the 02xx section). The latter is newer, so this character has been "disambiguated" at some point. Their glyphs should be named as they are specified in standard.nam, and both should use commaaccent in the design. 3. The issue of Scedilla and Scommaaccent is a bit more complicated. Again, in older versions of the Standard there was just one character that was meant for use in both Turkish and Romanian. Later, the character has been disambiguated. The Latin capital letter S with cedilla should use the cedilla in the design, and the Latin capital letter S with commaaccent should use the commaaccent. For Romanian however, some locales use the old (wrong) S with cedilla Unicode rathen than the new assignment. Therefore, it is useful to provide an OpenType "locl" feature that replaces Scedilla with Scommaaccent in the Romanian context. (All above comments also apply to lowercase appropriately, of course). Some more discussions: [http://groups.msn.com/FontLab/general.msnw?action=get\_message&ID\_Message...](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/groups.msn.com/FontLab/general.msnw?action=get_message&ID_Message=1825 "http://groups.msn.com/FontLab/general.msnw?action=get\_message&ID\_Message=1825") Adam

Anonymous @ 26 Jan 2007 — 3:10am
William, for what it matters, a large part of the Eastern European or Central European countries that use Latin accented characters outside of the Western set are part of the European Union now. The situation looked very different in the 1990s but these days, my sense is that for example in Poland, font piracy is even less imminent than in Germany. This has to do with a smaller number of fonts that have these characters and with a smaller number of font distributors, so if an ad or a magazine uses an illegal copy of a font, it’s pretty easy to spot this. This has also to do with factual enforceability of copyright, which is actually quite amazing. A few years ago, Business Software Alliance seized computers in some companies and actual led to some convictions (not for illegitimate fonts but for illegal software installations), which had a large impact on the publishing industry. To some extent, it has also to do with the fairly low typographic awareness of the clients and designers -- designers are keen on trying out new fonts and alternate solutions, but are not so extremely determined to do so. The clients won’t be extremely upset if the designer uses a more common font rather than a more original one. So the designer will only go for something different if he is sure that the client will pay for it and that everything is in the legitimate area. Since many fonts don’t come equipped with CE glyphs, local font distributors such as Kentype in Warsaw "localize" the fonts, i.e. add the local glyphs. Typographically, this is not always the best option (because many of those "localizations" are done hastily) but some font distributors actually put the client’s name at the end of the copyright string in the modified font. This has proven to be a quite good method to encourage the clients not to leak the fonts outside of their company. Since these are custom localizations that the clients pay extra for (though it’s not really much), the clients actually do have a slight sense of exclusivity of what they have, so they’re less likely to leak the fonts anyway. Given that nearly half of European Union uses Latin characters outside of the Western codepage, I think it only make sense to "pre-emptively" put them in, especially that with FontLab Studio, the Generate Glyphs feature, anchors and class kerning, it’s not really a very complex task. As for the design of the diacritic characters goes, there are many resources available online, e.g.: [Filip Blažek’s Diacritics project](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/diacritics.typo.cz/) [My old Polish how-to document](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/www.twardoch.com/download/polishhowto/intro.html) (needs to be re-worked) [My essay about Polish diacritics](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/www.twardoch.com/adam/project.php?pid=0016) [Victor Gaultney’s essays](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/www.sil.org/~gaultney/research.html) [Slides from a lecture Victor and I did](http://web.archive.org/web/20150326031624/http:/www.twardoch.com/adam/project.php?pid=0062) People like myself offer critique and consulting on diacritic design for particular projects, both paid and unpaid, depending on the nature of the project. Best, Adam
