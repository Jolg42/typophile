_Your theory of his reasons for returning to classical typography conflict with mine however. Do you have source on that. I am very interested in him and his works. Also, as you know, Tschichold and I had a mutual friend._ In what I've read of Tschichold's own writing, he never rejects asymetric typography; rather, he rejects his early ideological insistence on asymetric typography, and acknowledges that both asymetric and classical typography have their place, with the latter being most appropriate for books. I think it is important to look at the kind of material Tschichold was designing at different points in his career, and that the move to classical typography coincides with a move from advertising and what we would now call 'identity' design (e.g. stationery) to book design -- and book design of a specific kind: predominantly literary texts in octavo format. I have an interesting German book that combines Tschichold's writings -- illustrated with samples of his work -- from both halves of his career. Most English language publications, excepting Ruari Maclean's not very good biography, deal with either his asymetric typography _or_ his classical book design, as determined by the interest/bias of the translator or publisher. The German book is better, I think, because it conveys the consistency of Tschichold's attention to detail and quality, and makes one realise how this, not apparently opposing styles, is really key to understanding what Tschichold was about. In his early career, he developed asymetric typography to a very high level, and wrote books urging other typographers to follow suit. In his later career, he applied exactly the same level of quality to classical typography, and wrote what amount to style guides that would enable other typographers to achieve pleasing results. This is why I think the relative ease of achieving such results in the classical style, compared the more difficult task of achieving beauty in asymetric typography, was an important factor in Tschichold's _volte face_, especially as it is expressed in pedagogical efforts such as the essays in _The form of the book._ The fact that he developed his classical book design techniques in the context of the Penguin publishing programme is also interesting, and suggests a context in which mass-production of attractive typography, achievable by designers and printers following style guides, would become preferable to more difficult works requiring individual genius of the kind Tschichold himself had demonstrated in his early career.

The first act is, in general, to hide the offender's composing stick; if this does not answer, his galleys are secreted; then the page cords, which secure his work, are cut, and his labour rendered more than useless, because he has to distribute his pie_ [spilled type] _as well as recompose his matter; if he still remains contumacious, the whole of the types in his case are transposed, so that he cannot proceed in his business; and if he should still set the chapel at defiance, he is smoked, all the members of the chapel surrounding his frame; each with a lighted match of brimstone, and singing a doleful ditty..._ And from Moxon's _Mechanick Exercises,_ 1683: _...and the Penalty for the breach of any of these Laws and Customs is in Printers Language called a_ Solace. _... But if the Delinquent prov'd Obstinate or Refractory, and would not pay his Solace at the Price of the Chappel, they_ Solac'd _him. The manner of Solacing, thus. The Workmen take him by force, and lay him on his Belly athwart the Correcting-stone, and held him there while another of the Work-men with a Paper-board, gave him_ 10l. and a purse_, viz. Eleven blows on his Buttocks; which he laid according to his own mercy. For Tradition tells us, that about 50 years ago was one Solaced with so much violence, that he presently Pissed Blood; and shortly after dyed of it._

Hi Brian I too very much like Bauhaus-inspired architecture, but I also loved Tom Wolfe's book. It's a really fascinating and well-developed argument. Another book you may like is Prince Charles' "A Vision of Britain: A Personal View of Architecture." Here's a couple of lines I got off Amazon's website about it: "The Prince's central point is that modern architecture has lost sight of its surroundings. Rather than creating structures that harmonize with their location, using local materials and respecting the history of the site, many modern buildings seem determined to draw attention to themselves -- or rather, to their architects. Like any art, architecture is a matter of taste. But while you can hide a bad statue or painting, an ugly building is a blot on the landscape that's darn hard to avoid." I read this book way back when it was published -- I think in the mid 1980s. It caused quite a stir at the time, but now his argument seems pretty sensible. The book also includes some fairly decent drawings by the prince himself. Troy
