Your favorite examples of Letter Tailoring

Letter Tailoring is when a logo is made or a sign painted and the letters are modified for a better fit.

Do you have a favorite example?

I have collected examples from Doyald Young and Gerard Huerta, but I would be interested in other examples too.

I am of course especially interested in those examples that might apply to text face design because they are subtle enough and or work with roman forms, but feel free to post your favorite even if it falls outside this range.

Here is a quote that Gerard Huerta was kind enough to give me. <cite>The Architectural Digest masthead was designed to be slightly condensed and<br> more tightly spaced than I would normally want. It is not a font, but a<br> hand-drawn logo. The purpose is to give it more presence on the cover. At<br> nineteen characters it is the one of the more complex mastheads.</cite> <cite>As we know a font’s characters are meant to all work with each other. In a<br> logo where we have fixed relationships we can draw the best possible<br> letterform combinations.</cite> <cite>There are two ligatures in the artwork: “AR” and “RAL.” In the first<br> ligature the leg (and angle) of the “R” is allowed to tuck nicely under the<br> “C.” In the second ligature the angle of the leg of the “R” has been changed<br> to allow the consistent spacing that has been established.</cite> <cite>There are two “C”s in the word “Architectural.” The second “C” has an<br> unfortunate neighbor “T”, and combined with the open space left by the “C”<br> it leaves more negative space than one would want in this situation.<br> Therefore, the ending of the “C” has been slightly extended and weight has<br> been added to that part of it to compensate for the excessive negative<br> space. </cite> <cite>The last thing to mention is the bookmatched serifs in the combinations<br> “CT”, “ES”, and “ST.”</cite> James, I will comment further on the Huemiller later today or the next.
