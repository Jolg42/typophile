Unfamiliar use of the em-dash

I came across something I haven’t seen before while reading To Kill a Mockingbird. It seems the writer – or perhaps editor – are using the em-dash as some kind of ellipsis. Here are two examples, the second one make use of both an em-dash and an ellipsis (and I think there should have been a space before the em, as in the first example).

“Mayella Violet Ewell —!” (Calling the witness to stand.)

There was something about them, no matter how much they cussed and drank and gambled and chewed; no matter how undelectable they were, there was something about them that I instinctively liked … they weren’t—
      “Hypocrites, Mrs. Perkins, born hypocrites,” Mrs. Merriweather was saying.

Can anyone more familiar with the language enlighten me on this?

Anonymous @ 17 Sep 2009 — 5:42pm
The use of the em-dash to signify a pause or trailing off at the end of a sentence may be an American phenomenon. Emily Dickinson used dashes everywhere in her handwritten manuscripts, and in some editions, editors left the poems that way rather than substituting more conventional punctuation. When you're writing with a pen or pencil, it's often easier to make a dash than to choose between a comma, semicolon, or ellipses. I don't know if Harper Lee wrote _To Kill a Mockingbird_ on a typewriter or not, but if she wrote in longhand, she may have simply defaulted to dashes, and her editor chose not to change them. I was always taught that in type, em-dashes should be "tight," but that may be discretionary, like the spacing after a period.
