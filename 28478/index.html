Anonymous @
Typewiz, you have mentioned a number of fonts that can work well. But I think you cannot make the best choice in the abstract. You need also to think about the page layout. Will you have large type or small? How long will the line be (measure)? How much leading will you use? And what is the mood of the subject matter, and the mood you want to convey as an author? Also, how will the final product be printed? By laser; by offset? The answers to these and other questions will tell you more about what face is most appropriate. Of course, these will interact with the faces you try: if you have latitude you can try different faces with different layouts to come up with a good solution. For example, I think that the small x-height of Adobe Garamond Pro makes it work best at larger text sizes. Oh, if you haven't read Bringhurst, do--you will find it a revelation.

\>14 word line With 5 letter words and spaces that is 84 characters. This way too long according to traditional typesetting wisdom, as explained by Bringhurst. The traditional agreed ideal for single column pages is about two a half lc alphabets, or 66 characters. "More than 75 or 80 characters is likely to be too long for continuous reading." Setting type on letter sized paper, if that is what you are doing, is a typographic challenge, and to meet it you need to use wide type, such as Century Schoolbook, and wide margins (or wide left margin) with titles or notes in the margin, or use a multi-column setting. The typeface is probably a lesser issue than the layout for a good result. As far as mood, I do indeed think this is appropriate to consider even in a text face. For example, Garamond gives a more traditional and Whitman a more modern impression. I think this character can enhance your message.

Anonymous @
You can reduce characters per line by: a. using a font that sets wider, ie has a longer lower case alphabet at a given size; b. increasing the size of type. c. shortening the line. That's about it. Many reports and wide formats, eg instruction books, have a wide left column in which they put headings and information. Or, as I said you can go multi-column. Another way that looks good is to have two book style pages on a landscape oriented page. But as Nick said, if your audience is your teacher only, then you'd better do something that looks good to him or her--and that likely means 'normal'. You can't really have control of layout in MS Word, just forget it. You can do better even with that, by controlling the face, length of line, spacing and margins in a single column, as well as nice headings, etc. But when it comes to putting stuff in the margins, multicolumn etc., Word is just torture. Even MS Publisher is better, though a proper layout program is the only way to go in the long run.
