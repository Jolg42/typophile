When I think of the history of French cinema, I think of _auteurs_ who were formally innovative—e.g. Melies, Gance, Cocteau, Godard, and now Hazanavicius. While it’s true that Cassandre’s Peignot was also formally innovative in its day, it has rather a traditional art deco look, even in the true unicase setting. Why not go with something post-modern that suggests innovation today? Pierre di Sciullo: [http://www.quiresiste.com/realisations.php?lang=en](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608/http:/www.quiresiste.com/realisations.php?lang=en "http://www.quiresiste.com/realisations.php?lang=en") Excoffon, 20th century master: [https://www.myfonts.com/person/Roger\_Excoffon/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608/https:/www.myfonts.com/person/Roger_Excoffon/ "https://www.myfonts.com/person/Roger\_Excoffon/")

A general specific french cinema style? Hardly to grasp. Let Google sort out images to “cinema français affiche” and you’ll see – everything. But looking for “cinema français affiche 1930” reveals a different scenery: type like [Marlowe](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/facetype/marlowe/) or the stunning [Mostra Nuova](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/marksimonson/mostra-nuova/) by – Mark Simonson. To him I’m especially grateful for his intricate review of type in the film The Artist. For a very long time I feel that lettering and type is awkwardly neglected by film makers, mostly. Mark’s article opened my eyes upon how crucial (!) script and lettering is for a silent movie in particular. ![](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608im_/http:/typophile.com/files/brain_4880.png) ![](http://web.archive.org/web/20150415100608im_/http:/typophile.com/files/rauchen_6502.png)
