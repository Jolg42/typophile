Turn of the twentieth century fonts

So, I have a ton of vintage type specimen books and wood type references, but I’m looking for some particular knowledge/advice. I’m working on the brand for a restaurant that is described as “John D. Rockefeller and Theodore Roosevelt enjoying a whiskey together.”
Obviously, there is a ton of reference to Victorian and Art Nouveau which would come right before and Bauhaus / De stijl which would come right after, but I’m trying to pinpoint if there is a typographic “style” that would have appeared in New York City in the year 1900. I’m trying to avoid “western” (wood type) and european (overly ornamental)…

any thoughts and resources/examples would be very much appreciated! Thank you!

Anonymous @ 16 Jan 2015 — 6:23pm
I take it that the cliche you want to avoid when you say "western" is (what used to be called) French Clarendon, the usual style identified with the Gay '90s - think Wendy's. But avoiding cliches and seeking true authenticity may go over the heads of a typographically unsophisticated public. However, something authentic probably will be recognized as old because people will remember stuff from old movies, old books, and so on - thus one really shouldn't be afraid of doing it right. Also, the other important thing to consider is that the restaurant is intended to suggest a very high-class and expensive restaurant of that time period, not just an ordinary restaurant. That eliminates some of the styles of lettering used in that period - limiting one to more tasteful ones, less likely to be as obviously dated.

Anonymous @ 18 Jan 2015 — 10:05am
The years around 1900 were marked by the the popularity of display fonts that imitated hand lettering. These would have been copied by sign painters. Perhaps one of the most successful was the style that became familiar with \_The Saturday Evening Post\_ name plate. This was produced as Post Old Style. This was a "rugged" font -- a style that today we see as distressed or grunge. Smoothed digital versions are available. Consider Saturday Morning Toast NF [http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/nicksfonts/saturday-morning-toast/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150414044716/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/nicksfonts/saturday-morning-toast/ "http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/nicksfonts/saturday-morning-toast/") and Lamp Post [http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/jnlevine/lamp-post/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150414044716/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/jnlevine/lamp-post/ "http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/jnlevine/lamp-post/") Don
Anonymous @ 18 Jan 2015 — 10:02am
BTW sign painters of the era avoided sans-serif like the plague, except for the most utilitarian or industrial usage. As an alternative to serifs with prominent serifs they might use tiny serifs and add some decoration such as a shadow line. This ethos is captured in a modern font, Horndon ![](http://web.archive.org/web/20150414044716im_/http:/typophile.com/files/HorndonAsREST_5014.gif) [http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/linotype/horndon/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150414044716/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/linotype/horndon/ "http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/linotype/horndon/") Don
