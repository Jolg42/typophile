Ascender releases its first OpenType Pro font

Submitted by Miss Tiffany : 19.Dec.2005 8.43am

PRESS RELEASE

Ascender releases its first OpenType Pro font

Pericles™ Pro fonts offer advanced typographic features

ELK GROVE VILLAGE, IL - December 19, 2005 - Ascender Corporation, a leading font development firm is pleased to announce the release of Pericles Pro, an OpenType font available for purchase and immediate download from the Ascender Font Store ( http://www.ascenderfonts.com).

—

Pericles Pro is an attractive typeface for headlines and short passages of text which recall inscriptional lettering and playful, art deco design. There are two weights of Pericles Pro, Light and Regular, containing OpenType-enabled typographic features with alternative characters for creating eye-popping effects in headlines, book titles, banners, and greeting cards.

“Pericles is a fun, yet versatile design”, said Steve Matteson, Ascender’s Type Director. “Users may pick and choose alternate letterforms to create graphic word-forms or simply use the normal characters depending on the effect they want to achieve. A little bit of playfulness goes a long way in creating unique, attention-getting messages”.

Pericles Pro is based on the work of Robert Foster who created the original for American Type Founders in 1934. Steve Matteson first saw Pericles in an old ATF specimen book with the description: ’combines the purity of the Greek with the virility of the modern’. The original included alternative designs for E, R and Q. Steve believed the design could be taken a great deal further by utilizing OpenType technology. In addition to the alternate letter designs, Pericles Pro also includes the accented characters necessary for setting languages supported by code pages Latin 1, 2 and 5.

Each Pericles Pro font includes 433 glyphs. This includes 12 stylistic alternates and 17 ligatures to mix and match with a full set of capitals, small capitals, superscript, subscript and petite small capital letters. Pericles Pro was developed to take advantage of the rich typographic OpenType features of applications Adobe Creative Suite, as well as Windows Presentation Foundation (WPF) and the forthcoming QuarkXPress 7. Each Pericles Pro font retails for $30.00 and is available for purchase and immediate download from the http://www.ascenderfonts.com website.

A PDF brochure of Pericles Pro can be viewed here: http://www.ascendercorp.com/pdf/pericles.pdf

About Ascender Corporation
Ascender Corporation is a leading provider of advanced font products specializing in font design, development and licensing. Ascender’s founders are font industry experts, and have been involved in developing some of the most important and influential fonts used in computers, phones and printers. Ascender provides multilingual, custom font development for enterprises and hardware & software developers. Ascender also provides high quality fonts for immediate download from the Ascender Font Store website.

Variation of stress between characters in Caslon

Hello fellow typophiles,

I was reading about Caslon in Anatomy of a Typeface and while looking at some samples I noticed that his lowercase o has a very vertical stress, while other letters such as p/q/d/b/e have a diagonal stress. This seems to change a little from size to size; in some the o stress is more vertical in others more diagonal.

I know that typefaces from earlier centuries had varying stress, but as far as I knew they varied inside a certain range, being always more or less diagonal.

I’m sure this must be something very obvious and common to most of you… but I just noticed it, and I’m left wondering if this is something characteristic of and/or first done by Caslon. My few books on type unfortunately don’t bring this up clearly.

Below is a partial view of the sample I’m looking at. It’s the Two Lines English from the 1763 specimen book, p 173 in Anatomy.

Thanks a lot for your help/opinions/insights/etc!

OpenType Superiors Feature: Why do letters and numbers have different baselines?

When comparing the “superiors” feature with the “fractions” feature in a number of OpenType fonts from Adobe and Linotype (and others?), some questions arise. Specifically, in the superior glyph range, why do letters have a different baseline from numbers and punctation? This is not the case with glyphs covered by the fractions feature.

Here are some examples illustrated:

Superior numbers have a higher baseline than superior letters. Why? (Illustrated above: [normal capital H], [superior 1], [superior 2], [superior 2], [superior 3], [superior a], [superior b], [normal lowercase c])

What this leads to, is impossible typography, as pictured above. The period should have the same baseline as the superior letter, no? Instead, it has the baseline of the numbers.

Fraction numbers are larger than superior numbers. This is understandable.

Fraction numbers and letters share a common baseline. This seems to make typographic sense. Why here and not with superiors?

Could this have something to do with a legacy work-around? If a type designer, or major type foundry, were to change this, would it cause any problems? Are there users who expect the superior feature to be defined in this manner?
