Colin Banks

Colin Banks (1932-2002) co-founder of Banks & Miles, designers and typographers, founded in London in 1958 with partner John Miles. Major clients included the Consumers Association, the Post Office, British Telecom and London Transport, for whom they redesigned Edward Johnston’s famous Underground Sans (as New Johnston). Banks would later design a limited edition book for the organisation as a tribute to Johnston.

An influential designer, his Telecom (T) identity, created for British Telecommunications when it was instituted in 1981, spawned many imitators. Its replacement by Wolff Olin’s BT ‘piper’ was received with much derision in 1991. He received a prestigious RSA/BBC Design Award in 1990, for the paper-saving redesign of the UK’s Phonebook. Banks designed the UK Post Office’s distinctive ‘double-line’ alphabet.

He was married to Caroline Grigson, daughter of the poet Geoffrey Grigson and his first wife.

French use of contextual "v"

Looking through French mathematics books from the 19th century, I see mostly this typeface. I assume it’s a standard Didot, but it has a more gentle thick/thin contrast than any Didone I see today. Also, it has a contextual italic-lc “v”, rounded at the beginning of words but pointed when alone, in equations, or within words. The particular sample in the picture also has a straight italic-lc “f” whereas one with a curved tail is the usual.

As far as I can tell, the sharp “v” was used regularly until about 1910. Here are my questions:

  1. What’s the story on the sharp “v”, that is, history and conventions of usage and why it ended?

  2. Is there a digitized version of this font that has the moderate thick/thin variation and the alternate “v”?

Letterspacing/kerning - how/what when why?

What is letterspacing?

How do you kern letters ‘by eye’?
Jim Krause in ‘Type Index’ on page 90 says to ’try focusing on sets of three (adjacent) letters at a time;’
‘squinting helps accentuate “dark spots” and “holes” within the word-’

‘Thinking in Type’ by Alex White on page 365 says to flip a headline upside down so it’s shapes become noticeable or by imagining equal amounts of water poured between characters.
In the example he gives he has the spaces between letters as grey on the x height of the letters. So you are supposed to compare unequal/strange shapes.
He uses ‘Avelas’ as an example. I am not sure why he doesn’t include the ascender or rather the parts of the letter that extends past the x height in the equal spacing calculation. Why does he not include the bowls and the negative space of the letters in the calculation (e.g. the ‘A’ has a space - the ‘counter’)? He provides a chart of most significant kerning pairs on page 364.

Would equal spacing be optically okay spacing? If you measured from the ‘A’ to the lowercase ‘v’ would that be the same for all letters?
