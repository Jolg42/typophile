Hi there, Ok, I don’t know if it exactly fits - it hasn’t exactly got rounded corners - but even if this is the wrong one, it’s a doozy, so I’ll post it anyway. The [Deutsche Blockschrift](http://web.archive.org/web/20081025051542/http:/isarsteve.de/blog/index.php?/archives/2006/10.html) was not a font, but hand lettering used in the 1920’s and 1930’s. It’s used in a bunch of the central subway stations in Berlin, most notably in a few old platforms at Alexanderplatz. I’ve always thought it definitely must have influenced Arno Drescher’s [Grotesk](http://web.archive.org/web/20081025051542/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/bitstream/drescher-grotesk-bt/familytree.html), which was also designed in the 30’s. After WWII, the (only) East German type foundry [VEB Typoart](http://web.archive.org/web/20081025051542/http:/pingmag.jp/2007/10/05/veb-typoart-the-east-german-type-betriebsstatte/) continued producing Drescher Grotesk, also called Super Grotesk, and it became one of their bestsellers. After the Wall fell and VEB Typoart dissolved, Sven Smital from Berlin design collective eBoy (known for their elaborate pixel drawings) did a [Super Grotesk revival](http://web.archive.org/web/20081025051542/http:/www.fontshop.com/fonts/downloads/fontfont/ff_super_grotesk/) for FontFont. Also interesting and German and partly from the 30’s are the [FF City Street Type](http://web.archive.org/web/20081025051542/http:/www.typophile.com/wiki/FF%252BCity%252BStreet%252BType)s, although they’re probably not what you’re looking for. The East Berlin version has rounded corners, but it’s from the 50’s. Good luck!

Anonymous @ 7.Dec.2007 11.01am
Could it be you mean Super-Grotesk schmalfett (bold condensed) in the german poster edgeonyou shows? The text is from the communist East Germany in the beginning of the 60ies, Cold War started already. And in East Germany Super-Grotesk was the most common Grotesk after all old east german foundries were overtaken in VEB TypoArt. They had no access to lead types from West Germany in the following decades.
