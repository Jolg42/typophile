"Antique" faces similar to [Bruce Old Style](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/spiecegraphics/zinc-italian-sg/) were popular, as was [De Vinne](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/bitstream/de-vinne/) and [Egyptian](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/bitstream/egyptian-710/). Faces like Egyptian would often be used as a boldface for De Vinne or Bruce Old Style. Ronaldson was also popular. Unfortunately, the closest thing available to it today is [Tiffany](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/linotype/itc-tiffany/), which doesn't really have the same antique feel (unless you're talking about 1970s antiques :-). Faces like the original (non-ITC) [Bookman](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/bitstream/bookman/) and [Cushing](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/bitstream/itc-cushing/) were common. Also [Romana](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/urw/romana/). I've got a children's history book from 1889 that uses [Art Gothic](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/urw/art-gothic/) for section headings. Jonathan Hoefler's [Knockout Series](http://web.archive.org/web/20050516143610/http:/www.typography.com/catalog/knockout/series_e.html) would not feel out of place. Of course, it's not just the type choices, but how you set them. I would recommend getting hold of or at least looking at books from the late 1800s to get layout ideas.

Anonymous @ Sat, 2004-05-22 19:33
Palatino is from about 1950 and was inspired by Renaissance calligraphy. It's definitely not a look I would ever associate with the late nineteenth century. Similarly, Garamond's fonts were made in the 1600s and were not revived until the 1920s. The late nineteenth century was big on "antiques," which evolved from Caslon's types, and moderns, such as De Vinne. (Caslon's original faces, which were from the 18th century, were not popular again until they were revived the early 20th century.) Old style, in the sense usually meant today, meaning things like Bembo, Jenson, Garamond, Caslon, etc., is a very 20th-century thing. Even a lot of the moderns, like Bodoni and Dido, and transitionals, like Baskerville and Bulmer, were out of style by the late 19th century and didn't make a comeback until the 1920s. Most of the faces we call "old style" now were considered obsolete in the late 1800s. If you look at books and other things published back then you will see what I mean.
