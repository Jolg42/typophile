Oldstyle miniscule a

I have fallen in love with typefaces with the scribally influenced miniscule a’s. They’re not wimpy or weak at all, and the way they’re squared off makes them seem rugged and virile to my eyes.

These types of lc a’s seem to be prevalent particularly in garalde and venetian oldstyle typefaces, but a few modern serifed fonts have used them as well. This seems to be a particular favorite form of Robert Slimbach, as quite a few of his typefaces have this particular style of a.

Nicholas Jenson types
LTC Metropolitan Pro ( Centaur)

ITC Legacy Serif

Adobe Jenson Pro

Claude Garamond types
Adobe Garamond Premier Pro

Stempel Garamond

20th Century types

Madrigal Antikva

Does anyone know anything else about these styles of miniscule a’s?

One of the major book romans with that style **a** is Bembo, based on the type cut by Francesco Griffo for Aldus Manutius’ 1495 edition of De Aetna by Pietro Bembo in Venice. [Adobe make a good Bembo](http://web.archive.org/web/20080101191801/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/adobe/bembo/) Here’s a picture: ![](http://web.archive.org/web/20080101191801im_/http:/typophile.com/files/anotherbembo_5390.gif) Compare it to Adobe’s and it becomes obvious the one I used here lacks authenticity, the bowl being too tall and not square enuff. Also look at [Monotype’s Poliphilus](http://web.archive.org/web/20080101191801/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/agfa/poliphilus/), used by Manutius to print the infamous Hypnerotomachia Polyphilus book of fantastic nonsense. _...the way they’re squared off makes them seem rugged and virile to my eyes._ Rigor, robustness, tension. The easiest way to get it is to make the curves and letter form squarish. Presently in some guises I guess it also goes by the name of “hyperellipse”. j a m e s
