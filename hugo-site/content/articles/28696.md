In this way, it is easier to etch or carve words, symbols, and hierogplyphs from the right to the left.  This right to left approach was almost universal; even the Latins and Greeks started at the right (this was a VERY long time ago). When pens and papyrus/vellum came along, the Greeks innovated by going in both directions within the same text; that is, they started from the right, got to the end of a line and then started going back toward the right, and then back again in a zig-zag. The Latins felt this was just too much and they settled on the left to right method.  In Asia, paper has been around for a very long time, as has ink; what came first, however, were turtle shells and bones upon which the ancient Chinese carved their earliest pictographs/ideograms. The nature of these materials (being thin) caused the writer to start carving at the top of the bone or shell and go to the bottom (this is a natural progression because of the height/width of Chinese characters).  When the Chinese invented paper, they continued the practice of top to bottom writing; but why they started at the right of the page is beyond me.
