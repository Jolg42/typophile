That version of the Bell System logo was first introduced in 1939, and persisted until Saul Bass created his iconic revision in 1969. “Public Telephone” resembles Akzidenz-Grotesk (the |C| especially), but your sample has a much rounder |O| than the one in A-G. Without knowing an approximate date for the sign, it’s hard to pin down the original face. These digital typefaces could help you approximate the look: [http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/marksimonson/proxima-nova/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150416122618/http:/www.myfonts.com/fonts/marksimonson/proxima-nova/ "http://www.myfonts.com/fonts/marksimonson/proxima-nova/") [http://www.google.com/fonts/specimen/Raleway](http://web.archive.org/web/20150416122618/http:/www.google.com/fonts/specimen/Raleway "http://www.google.com/fonts/specimen/Raleway") [http://www.typography.com/fonts/gotham/overview/](http://web.archive.org/web/20150416122618/http:/www.typography.com/fonts/gotham/overview/ "http://www.typography.com/fonts/gotham/overview/")

This icon--which achieved a remarkable 93 percent recognition rate in the United States [emphasis added] --aspired to the simplicity and directness of a sans serif letterform. In 1984, the familiar bell symbol was transferred to the divested "Baby Bells," and Bass & Yager designed a striated sphere for AT&T, aiming to signify the corporation's international stature and the ascendance of digital communications." The AT&T Long Lines Department was responsible for connecting the local Bell companies together to provide long distance telephone communication between each Bell company.
