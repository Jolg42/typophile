Author requested permission to use my poster in his book, should I ...?

I received and email from an art history professor who’s writing a book on poster design, and he wants to include one of my posters. He purchased one directly from me off the internet a year ago. I’ve never had such a request before, and I’m not quiet sure how to reply.

He hasn’t offered any compensation, just asked for permission and a short bio to accompany the poster. He has a contract with a major publisher already, so I assume he’ll profit (off the work of several designers!)

Should I reply with a payment request, or just allow him to use it for the PR?
Is he responsible for licensing the fonts I used in the poster?

Has anyone else been published like this? If so, has it been beneficial or not?

Thanks for any advice you can offer.

Anonymous @ 5.May.2009 5.19am
If it’s a 186 poster book with 200 pages, there could easily be a solid 30-60 pages of writing, which could indeed be a pretty serious bit of writing. While when I write an academic work on literature I can easily reference multiple works citing only the most relevant part since presumably any major library at a research university will have them or can quickly retrieve them with ILL, pieces on visual works (especially posters which can be have very short public lives and may not otherwise be published in any other place), illustrations are a necessity and may not be available in published form anywhere. I’d discuss the work some with the author, to see precisely how he wants to go about the work. If it’s a mere collection, then you’ll likely want to ask for compensation, but (I think I lean more to this) if it’s a substantial work that requires illustrations to be meaningful, I don’t think it’s necessary. Actually, just ask him to see a draft copy of the chapter he’s using your book and maybe the introduction too. Contractual obligations with the publisher aside, he’ll probably let you take a look, and from what you read you can decide how to proceed. Liberal arts publishing, at least from my experience in the literature world, isn’t quite as secretive as, say, science publishing. _«El futuro es una línea tan fina que apenas nos damos cuenta de pintarla nosotros mismos». Anonymous @ 5.May.2009 5.22am
Alaskan, maybe I can cite a couple of examples from the type community... First example: Tamye Riggs recently posted a request here (and [elsewhere](http://web.archive.org/web/20090808194713/http:/www.facebook.com/group.php?gid=35678162623)) for work using certain typefaces considered to be classic, and [the book is due to be published in June](http://web.archive.org/web/20090808194713/http:/www.rotovision.com/description.asp?isbn=978-2-88893-043-3). The other example: When Leslie Cabarga was writing his [_Logo, Font and Lettering Bible_](http://web.archive.org/web/20090808194713/http:/www.lesliecabarga.com/products/logo-font-and-lettering-bible), he sent out requests to designers he knew, and he got many illustrations for his book this way. Everyone received credit, and got some exposure in exchange. A win-win situation for all involved, I suppose. I think that this author has gone about things the right way — he’s written you in advance to request your permission to use your work. Of course, you don’t know this professor the way you might know Tamye or Leslie, but you could find out if he’s published other books — that might give you some reassurance as to his intentions. Or, as Matthew suggests, ask him if you can see a draft, a chapter, etc.
